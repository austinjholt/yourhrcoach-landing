How to Fire an Employee Legally (Small Business Guide)

A step-by-step process for terminating an employee lawfully, respectfully, and with the documentation that protects your business.

By Dr. Steve Cohen  ·  HR Solutions On Call  ·  Last updated October 2026

Short answer: To fire an employee legally, confirm you have a lawful, documented business reason; check that the decision isn't discriminatory or retaliatory; review contracts and policies; hold a short, private meeting with a witness; pay final wages on your state's deadline; provide required notices (like COBRA); and confirm everything in a written termination letter.

Written and reviewed by Dr. Steve Cohen, Ed.D., founder of HR Solutions On Call, 40+ years of HR consulting · Last updated October 2026

Most firings that end up in front of a lawyer, an unemployment hearing officer, or the EEOC don't go wrong in the meeting. They go wrong weeks earlier — when the reason wasn't documented, the warnings weren't written, or the timing looked like retaliation. After 40+ years of HR consulting, I can tell you the legal part of firing someone is mostly preparation. Here's the process I walk small business owners through.

Important: This is general guidance, not legal advice. State and local laws vary — on final pay, required notices, and what counts as wrongful termination. If the employee recently complained, took protected leave, filed a workers' comp claim, or is in a protected class and the facts are thin, talk to an employment attorney before you act.

Can I fire an employee for any reason?

In every state except Montana, employment is presumed to be at-will: you can end it for any lawful reason, or no stated reason, with or without notice. Montana's Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act generally requires good cause once an employee has completed a probationary period.

"At-will" does not mean "any reason." You cannot fire someone because of:

  • A protected characteristic — race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and gender identity), national origin, age 40+, disability, or genetic information under federal law, plus whatever your state adds. Federal anti-discrimination laws enforced by the EEOC generally apply at 15+ employees (20+ for age); many state laws apply to much smaller employers.
  • Retaliation — for complaining about discrimination, harassment, wage issues, or safety; for filing a workers' comp claim; or for taking FMLA leave. Retaliation is consistently the most frequently alleged basis in charges filed with the EEOC, according to the agency's own annual data.
  • Protected activity — jury duty, military service (USERRA), voting, whistleblowing, or discussing pay and working conditions with coworkers (protected by the National Labor Relations Act, even in non-union workplaces).
  • A contract or policy promise — an employment agreement, union contract, or handbook language that promises termination only "for cause" or after specific steps.

How do I fire an employee legally, step by step?

1. Confirm the real reason — and that you can prove it

Write one or two sentences stating why this person is being let go: "Failed to meet the sales targets in the 60-day improvement plan dated March 3" or "Violated the cash-handling policy on May 12, confirmed by register records." If you can't write it plainly, or the evidence is your memory, you're not ready.

2. Pull the documentation

Gather performance reviews, written warnings, improvement plans, attendance records, and any policy the employee acknowledged. For performance problems, the file should show the employee was told what was wrong, what needed to change, and by when. For serious misconduct (theft, violence, harassment), a single well-documented incident and a fair investigation can be enough.

3. Run a risk check

Ask these questions honestly before you decide:

  • Has the employee complained about anything, requested an accommodation, or taken leave in the last several months?
  • Is the employee in a protected class, and have others outside that class done the same thing without being fired?
  • Do any recent reviews say "meets expectations" while you now say performance is poor?
  • Does an offer letter, contract, or handbook promise a particular process?
  • Is the timing awkward — right after a pregnancy announcement, an injury, or a wage complaint?

A "yes" doesn't mean you can't proceed. It means slow down, make sure the documentation is airtight, and consider getting legal advice.

4. Decide on the details before the meeting

Settle the effective date, final paycheck timing, how unused PTO is handled under your policy and state law, whether you'll offer severance (and if so, in exchange for a signed release), benefit end dates, and how company property and system access will be handled.

5. Hold a short, private termination meeting

Meet in person or by video, early in the week if possible, with a second manager as a witness. Keep it to 10–15 minutes. State the decision in the first minute, give the brief reason, explain next steps, and end the meeting. Don't debate.

6. Pay final wages on time

Federal law (the FLSA) doesn't require immediate final pay, but many states do. California, for example, requires final wages at the time of termination when an employer fires someone; other states allow until the next regular payday. Some states also treat earned, unused vacation as wages that must be paid out. Check your state labor department's rules before the meeting.

7. Provide required notices

Depending on your size and state, this may include COBRA continuation notices (federal COBRA applies to employers with 20+ employees; many states have "mini-COBRA" laws for smaller groups), state-specific separation or unemployment notices, and written confirmation of the termination. If you're laying off 100+ employees, the federal WARN Act may require 60 days' notice.

8. Follow up in writing and close the file

Give the employee a termination letter confirming the effective date, final pay, benefits, and property return. Write a short memo to file of what was said in the meeting, who attended, and when. Then cut system access, notify payroll, and collect equipment.

What should I say when firing an employee?

Be direct, brief, and kind. A script that works in most situations:

Sample termination meeting script

"[Name], thanks for meeting with us. I have a difficult message: we've decided to end your employment, effective today.

As we discussed in [the warning on DATE / your improvement plan], [one-sentence factual reason]. The decision is final.

Here's what happens next: your final paycheck will be [timing/method]. Information on your benefits and COBRA will [be mailed / is in this packet]. We'll need your [keys, laptop, badge] today. This letter summarizes everything.

I know this is hard news. Do you have any questions about the logistics?"

Don't say: "This is hard for me too," "It's not personal," "We're going in a younger direction," "Upper management made me," or anything that adds new reasons not in the file. Every extra sentence is something that can be quoted back to you.

Do I have to give a reason for firing an employee?

In most at-will states, you're not legally required to state a reason. But giving a short, truthful, documented reason is almost always the better practice. Silence invites the employee — and later a lawyer or agency — to fill in the blank with a discriminatory motive. A handful of states also require employers to provide a written reason or "service letter" on request.

Should I offer severance?

Severance isn't required by federal law. It's useful when there's any legal risk, because you can condition it on a signed release of claims. If the employee is 40 or older, the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act requires specific release terms, including 21 days to consider the agreement (45 days in a group layoff) and 7 days to revoke after signing. Have an attorney review any release.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fire an employee without a warning?

In at-will states, yes, legally. But for performance problems, firing without prior written warnings makes the decision harder to defend. Serious misconduct like theft, violence, or harassment can justify immediate termination after a fair investigation.

What is the best day to fire an employee?

Many HR professionals prefer early in the week, so the employee can begin job searching and contact resources immediately rather than sitting with the news over a weekend. Avoid timing that coincides with a complaint, leave request, or medical disclosure.

How quickly do I have to give a fired employee their final paycheck?

It depends on your state. Some states, such as California, require final pay at the time of termination; others allow until the next regular payday. Federal law does not set a deadline. Check your state labor department's rules before the termination meeting.

Can a fired employee collect unemployment?

Usually, yes, unless the termination was for misconduct as defined by your state. Poor performance alone typically doesn't disqualify someone. Each state decides eligibility, and the employer can respond to the claim with documentation.

Should someone else be in the room when I fire an employee?

Yes. Have a second manager or HR contact attend as a witness and note-taker. It protects both you and the employee and gives you a reliable record of what was said.

Can I fire an employee who is on medical leave?

Firing someone on FMLA or other protected leave is high-risk because it can look like interference or retaliation. It can be lawful for reasons unrelated to the leave, such as a documented layoff, but get legal advice first.

Do I need a lawyer to fire an employee?

Not for every termination. A routine, well-documented termination often doesn't need one. Consult an employment attorney when the employee recently complained, took protected leave, requested accommodation, or when you're offering severance in exchange for a release.

What documents should I give a terminated employee?

Typically a termination letter, final pay information, benefits and COBRA information, any state-required separation or unemployment notice, and a reminder of continuing obligations such as confidentiality agreements.
